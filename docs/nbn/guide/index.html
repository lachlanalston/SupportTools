Read your NBN tests like they should have explained them

Every RSP can run diagnostics against your NBN service: line state, loopback, NTD status, flap counts. The results come back full of numbers, and when you ask support what they mean you get "it's within spec". This site decodes every field, gives you the thresholds that matter, and tells you when a result is normal, marginal, or worth a fight.

FTTNFTTBFTTCHFCFTTPFWSAT

How these tests get run in the first place

All of these diagnostics live in nbn's wholesale systems. Your RSP calls them through its own portal and shows you some or all of the output. Aussie Broadband exposes most of them for self-service in MyAussie (and in Carbon for business/wholesale customers); other RSPs run the same tests when you ask support, whether or not they show you the raw results. The tests are the same underneath regardless of who your RSP is, because nbn owns the access network.

Two things follow from that. First, this guide applies whatever provider you use. Second, when a support agent reads you a result over the phone, you can ask for the specific fields by name. They exist, even if the agent's screen summarises them away.

Most tests here are service impacting: they can briefly interrupt or resync the connection while they run. Run them outside anything latency-critical, and expect a minute or two of disruption on the intrusive ones.

The 60 second triage, any technology

StepQuestionIf no
1Does the RSP see the service up at all (check connection / port status / NTD status)?It is a connectivity fault: premises equipment, power, wiring or the nbn segment. Speed analysis is irrelevant until this passes.
2Does a loopback pass?Data is not flowing both ways. Power cycle the nbn equipment and CPE, rerun, then escalate. See the loopback test.
3Is the service stable (no dropout pattern, STABLE flag, low flap count)?Chase stability first, speed second. nbn's own threshold: 4 or more unexpected dropouts in 24 hours is a Performance Incident they are expected to investigate.
4Do the line metrics support the plan tier (sync at cap, attainable above tier, margins healthy)?Either the line cannot do the tier (a commercial problem, see what "in spec" means) or it has degraded (a fault, bring baselines).
5All of the above clean but the user reports slow?The access network is exonerated. Look at Wi-Fi, the router, in-premises cabling, the measuring device, VPNs, or upstream congestion. Test wired, at the first socket or the NTD.

Line State Interpreter FTTNFTTB

Paste in the numbers from a Line State test and get a per-field verdict using the thresholds documented on this page. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

FTTNFTTB The Line State test, field by field

FTTN/FTTB is VDSL2 over copper from a street node (or building MDF), so it is the one technology where the physical line has rich, continuously measured state. The Line State test is a snapshot of the DSLAM port as the node sees it. The modem must be in sync for the results to mean anything.

FieldWhat it isHow to read it
Operational StatusWhether the node port has DSL sync with the modemUP REQUIRED Down with the modem powered points at premises wiring, the modem, or a port/jumpering fault, not speed.
Service Stabilitynbn's classification from resyncs and errors over the recent windowSTABLE is the pass. Anything else is actionable: apply a stability profile, wait 48 hours, retest, escalate if unchanged.
MAC AddressThe modem the port currently seesConfirms the right premises is on the right pair. Useful in jumpering disputes and to prove a swapped modem connected.
DSL ModeModulation in useVDSL2 is correct. ADSL fallback means the CPE is misconfigured or negotiating badly.
Estimated Distance to NodeDerived from loop attenuation, not a measured cable runIndicative, but the best single predictor of what the line can do. See the distance table below.
Physical ProfileThree facts in one string: tier, DLM state, spectrum state. Example: 100/40 Standard 6dB (Co-Existence)Standard 6dB is the clean-history default. A Stability variant or 9dB/12dB target means the line has a dropout history and DLM or a tech intervened. (Co-Existence) describes the area, not a fault.
Attainable RateWhat the line could sync at right now given length, noise and profileCompare to the tier. Comfortably above: the copper supports the plan with headroom. Below the tier: the line physically cannot deliver the plan.
Current Sync RateThe rate the port and modem actually negotiatednbn caps sync roughly 10% above the tier (100/40 caps near 110/44, 50/20 near 55/22) so overheads still leave full speed at layer 3. Sync at the cap means the plan is fully available on the copper.
Loop AttenuationSignal loss across the copper, in dB. Scales with distanceLower is better. Single digits to low tens on short FTTN loops. Generic DSL bands: under 20 outstanding, 20 to 30 excellent, 30 to 45 workable, beyond that expect low tiers only.
Noise Margin (SNR)Headroom between received signal quality and the minimum needed to hold syncThe target is the profile number (6dB standard). Far above target with sync at cap: coasting, excellent. At or under 6dB and falling: dropout risk. Big day/night swings: noise ingress (REIN, crosstalk).
Attenuation AverageAggregate counterFrequently returns NaN or N/A because the DSLAM does not report it. A portal quirk, not a fault. Same for mislabelled units like "Mbps" on attenuation or "dBdB".

Distance rules of thumb

Community calibration rather than published nbn constants, and co-existence drags every band down:

Copper distanceRealistic attainableComment
Under 200 m100+ Mbps down100/40 territory. At 50 m, expect huge margins and a capped sync.
200 to 400 m60 to 100 Mbps100 tier possible but marginal at the far end.
400 to 700 m25 to 60 Mbps50 tier gets marginal, 25 comfortable.
Beyond 700 mOften under 25 MbpsThese are the lines the ACCC remedies exist for.

DLM and the profile string

nbn runs Dynamic Line Management on FTTN/B. A line that drops or errors too much gets moved toward stability: higher target noise margin (6 to 9 to 12 dB), interleaving and/or retransmission (G.INP). Each step trades speed (and a few ms of latency, for interleaving) for holding sync. Reading Standard 6dB is therefore itself diagnostic: nobody, human or DLM, has needed to intervene. A stability profile applied by the RSP does the same thing on request, and the standard workflow expects it: apply, hold 48 hours, rerun Line State, escalate only if still unstable.

Co-existence, and why it matters less than it sounds

(Co-Existence) means legacy services (ADSL2+ from the exchange, special services) still share copper in the area, so the node runs restricted spectrum and downstream power back-off to avoid interfering with them. It lowers attainable rates, mostly on longer loops, and it ends only when nbn is satisfied the legacy services are gone. Plenty of areas never formally exit. On a 50 m loop the effect is negligible; on a 600 m loop it can be the difference between holding a 50 tier and not.

Dropout arithmetic. nbn's operative threshold (introduced for HFC in Feb 2022, aligning with the existing FTTN metric): 4 or more unexpected dropouts in a 24 hour period is a Performance Incident, expected to produce a ticket and, if needed, a truck roll. Historically nbn called an average of ~2.4 resyncs/day "stable" and up to 5/day merely "risky", so if support waves away a service dropping several times daily, the PI threshold is the number to quote.

FTTC DPU and NCD tests

FTTC runs fibre to a Distribution Point Unit (DPU) in the pit outside, then VDSL2 over a short copper lead-in to the NCD (the nbn box inside). The twist: the DPU is reverse powered by the NCD over the copper pair. No powered NCD, no live DPU. That one fact explains most FTTC diagnostics.

TestWhat it doesHow to read it
DPU StatusChecks the pit equipment is onlineOffline usually means it is not being powered: NCD off, faulty, or the copper pair between them is broken. Check the NCD's lights and power before anything else.
DPU Port StatusVerifies power delivery from the NCD and shows the connected data rateThe lead-in is short, so the sync rate should sit near the maximum for the tier, comfortably above it. A low rate on a short lead-in points at the copper pair or a joint, and is escalation-worthy.
DPU Port ResetResets the pit-side portUse for offline status or a low data rate, then retest. Service impacting.
NCD UNI-D StatusChecks the Ethernet port your router plugs intoOperationally up but no data: do an NCD Port Reset. Down: check cabling and the router end.
NCD Reset / Port ResetForced reboot / port lock-unlock cycleThe standard first moves for dataflow issues before escalating. Service impacting.
FTTC has little tuning surface: the copper run is tens of metres. Persistent dropouts are usually the DPU losing power (NCD power environment, faulty NCD) or a degraded lead-in pair, both nbn-side fixes once you have evidence resets do not hold.

HFC NTD status and flap counts

HFC is the cable network: a shared coax segment into a cable modem style NTD. You get less visibility than copper DSL, and the key health signal is the flap count: how often the NTD's connection has interrupted and re-registered.

TestWhat it doesHow to read it
NTD StatusOperational check on the nbn connection boxFail: confirm power, then reset. Persistent fail with power is an nbn fault.
Flap countConnection interruption counter reported with statusTake a reading, wait 24 hours, take another. A commonly used escalation heuristic: 5 or more new flaps between tests a day apart is evidence of instability worth a fault. It also lines up with nbn's 4+ dropouts in 24h Performance Incident threshold.
NTD ResetForces the box to reboot and re-registerFirst move for errors; if status still reports problems after reset plus 10 minutes, escalate.
LoopbackBidirectional data path checkSee the loopback test.

What you cannot see: RF signal levels, noise on the coax segment, and neighbourhood congestion. Those live with nbn field techs. Your leverage is the dropout/flap evidence trail: timestamps, counts 24 hours apart, and the PI threshold. In-premises, check the coax fly lead and wall plate, and remove any old pay TV splitters, which are a classic silent killer of HFC signal.

FTTP UNI-D and port tests

Full fibre. There is no line quality to argue about in the copper sense: light either arrives within tolerance or it does not, and optical levels are visible to nbn rather than through RSP portals. What you can test is the service path and the data port.

TestWhat it doesHow to read it
UNI-D StatusChecks the specific Ethernet port on the NTD your service is provisioned onShows a MAC address when it can see your router. No MAC across two different routers: the port or provisioning is the problem, escalate. Remember there are four UNI-D ports and only the provisioned one works.
Port ResetLock/unlock cycle on the UNI-DFirst move for "port up but no data". Retest after two minutes.
LoopbackBidirectional data path checkSee the loopback test.

On FTTP, if the UNI-D shows your MAC, loopback passes, and speeds are still low, the copper-era instincts do not apply: it is almost never the access line. Look at CPE throughput limits (old routers that cannot route the tier), Wi-Fi, PPPoE vs IPoE config, CGNAT/VPN overlays, or RSP/upstream congestion, and speedtest wired from the NTD directly.

FW Fixed Wireless: different physics

Fixed Wireless is LTE-derived radio from a tower to an outdoor antenna (ODU). There is no line state equivalent in RSP portals; health is about radio conditions and cell load, and much of the diagnosis is pattern based.

SignalWhat to checkHow to read it
Connection box lightsPower and Status LEDs on the indoor unitGreen is normal. Amber/red power or red status indicates a fault state for the provider. No lights: power path.
Time-of-day patternSpeedtests morning, afternoon, evening over several daysGood all day except evenings: cell congestion, a capacity problem to raise with the RSP, not a fault in your equipment. Bad at all hours: signal path problem.
EnvironmentLine of sight to the towerGrown vegetation, new buildings, antenna knocked out of alignment, damaged cabling (wind, branches, rodents). Seasonal foliage and some summer weather measurably degrade cells.
Weather correlationNote whether drops track storms/heatConsistent weather correlation is useful evidence for an nbn investigation of the radio path.

Escalation on FW is evidence-driven: a diary of speeds by time of day, plus any physical observations about the antenna and line of sight, gets a signal investigation moving far faster than "it's slow".

SAT Sky Muster satellite, briefly

Geostationary satellite: the physics set expectations. Round-trip latency of roughly 600 ms is normal and no test will improve it; interactive workloads (VoIP, VPNs, remote desktop) behave accordingly. Heavy rain at your site or the ground station fades the signal (rain fade), so weather-correlated slowdowns are expected behaviour rather than faults. Persistent hard faults come down to dish alignment, cabling, or the modem, which is an nbn service call. Portal diagnostics are limited to connectivity checks, so the triage table at the top is most of what you can do remotely.

The loopback test, on every technology

The loopback sends a small amount of test data from the RSP/nbn side to your premises equipment and back, in both directions. On FTTN/B it exercises the layer 2 path across the POI, the node, the copper and the modem (which must be in sync); on the other technologies it loops via the NTD/NCD.

Passed means exactly one thing: at test time, data flowed both ways. It says nothing about speed, noise, or whether the service drops every evening. Intermittent faults routinely pass loopbacks, so a pass does not close a dropout investigation.

Failed while the port shows up and in sync is a meaningful contradiction: power cycle the nbn equipment and CPE, rerun, and escalate if it still fails. Expect the test to briefly interrupt traffic; a run typically takes a minute or two from created to completed.

What "meets the NBN spec" actually means

This is the part support teams wave at without explaining. The floors nbn commits to are much lower than the plan tier, which is why "it's within spec" and "your 100/40 service does 40" can both be true statements about the same line.

CommitmentFigureContext
FTTN during co-existence12/1 MbpsThe committed peak information rate objective while legacy services share the copper. nbn has disputed the word "cap" but stands by the figure as the commitment.
FTTB during co-existence25/5 MbpsBuilding copper is short, so the floor is higher.
After co-existence25/5 Mbps objectiveAligning with the statutory requirement of 25 Mbps peak downstream.
Dropouts, FTTN and HFC4+ unexpected in 24 hPerformance Incident: nbn is expected to investigate, ticket, and if needed roll a truck.

So when a tech says a poor line "meets spec", they usually mean it clears 12/1 or 25/5, not that it can do the plan you pay for. Arguing the plan tier with nbn is mostly futile. The plan-tier remedy sits with your RSP instead: under the ACCC's broadband speed claims guidance, RSPs must check a line's maximum attainable speed after activation, tell the customer if it cannot reach the plan tier, and offer a remedy (move to a lower tier with a refund, or exit without penalty). The Telstra and Optus undertakings, which compensated tens of thousands of FTTN customers, are the precedent to cite.

When nbn will act on a fault: the service is unstable past the PI threshold and a stability profile has not fixed it; the attainable rate has degraded materially against the line's own history; the line is below the floors above; or there is physical-layer evidence such as a collapsing noise margin or errors. Note the recurring theme: history wins arguments. A single bad snapshot is dismissible; the same line measurably worse than its own baseline is not.

Escalating properly

Support triage rewards specifics. The difference between "the internet is slow" and a ticket that moves is quoting the fields on this page back with numbers and dates.

Phrases that work

SituationWhat to say
Instability (FTTN)"Line State shows UNSTABLE with N resyncs. Please apply a stability profile; I will retest in 48 hours and want an nbn instability fault raised if it has not returned to STABLE."
Dropouts (any tech)"I have logged N dropouts in 24 hours with timestamps, which exceeds nbn's Performance Incident threshold of 4. Please raise it with nbn as a PI."
Line cannot do the tier"Attainable is X/Y on a 100/40 service. The line cannot support the tier, so please run the speed review process: I want the ACCC remedy options, and an nbn investigation only if this is a degradation from previous attainable figures."
Degradation"Attainable was A/B on [date] (attached) and is now C/D on the same profile. That is a physical degradation, please lodge it with nbn."
Clean line, slow experienceDo not burn an nbn ticket. Note the capped sync and healthy margins, then work the premises: wired test at the first socket or NTD, CPE throughput, Wi-Fi, VPN overlays.

Build a baseline habit

Snapshot test results when the service is healthy (at activation, after any nbn work) and every time a fault is suspected. Screenshots with dates. Degradation against a baseline is the single most persuasive artefact you can hand an RSP, because it converts "customer says it is slow" into "the access line measurably changed". For suspected noise or interference on copper, capture one business-hours test and one evening test and compare margins.

Glossary

Attainable rate
The maximum rate the line could sync at right now, given its length, noise environment and profile. The line's potential, as opposed to what it is doing.
Sync rate (line rate)
The rate the DSLAM port and modem actually negotiated. Capped by nbn a little above the tier so overheads still leave the full plan speed at layer 3.
Noise margin / SNR margin
Headroom, in dB, between the received signal quality and the minimum needed to hold the current sync. The profile sets a target (6dB standard); the DSLAM trades speed to hold it.
Loop attenuation
Signal loss across the copper loop in dB. Grows with distance and frequency; the basis for the estimated distance figure.
DLM
Dynamic Line Management. nbn automation that watches errors/resyncs and moves misbehaving lines to more conservative profiles (higher target margin, interleaving, G.INP).
G.INP
Physical-layer retransmission. Corrects errored DSL frames without the latency cost of deep interleaving.
Interleaving
Spreads data across time so error bursts are correctable. Costs a few ms of latency; a sign the line needed help.
Co-existence / DPBO
Spectrum restrictions (downstream power back-off) while legacy ADSL/special services share the copper. Lowers attainable rates, mostly on long loops.
Performance Incident (PI)
nbn's threshold for dropout investigation: 4 or more unexpected dropouts on a service in 24 hours.
DPU
Distribution Point Unit. The FTTC kerb device in the pit, reverse powered from the premises NCD.
NCD / NTD
Network Connection Device (FTTC) / Network Termination Device (FTTP, HFC). The nbn box in the premises.
UNI-D
The user Ethernet data port on an NTD/NCD. FTTP NTDs have four; only the provisioned one is live.
POI
Point of Interconnect. Where RSP networks meet the nbn access network; the far end of what these tests exercise.
TC4
Traffic class 4: best-effort data, i.e. normal broadband. What all of this applies to.
REIN
Repetitive Electrical Impulse Noise. Interference (pumps, lights, dodgy power supplies) that eats noise margin, often on a schedule.
