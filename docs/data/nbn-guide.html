NBN Field Guide

Every field of every test, in the order the portal shows them.

Line State

FTTN / FTTBservice impacting

A snapshot of the node port as the DSLAM sees your line. The modem must be in sync or the numbers mean nothing. Read top to bottom; the result is a story, not a scorecard.

Operational Status

Whether the node port currently has DSL sync with the modem.

Up before anything else matters. Down with the modem powered points at premises wiring, the modem itself, or the port; it is a no-sync problem, never a speed problem.

Service Stability

nbn's classification of the line from resyncs and errors over the recent window.

STABLE is the pass. Anything else, count dropouts yourself: 4 or more unexpected drops in 24 hours is a Performance Incident nbn is expected to investigate. Under that, it's copper being copper.

why

nbn's own historical numbers called ~2.4 resyncs a day "stable" and up to 5 "risky". The PI threshold (introduced 2022) is the operative line: it obliges a ticket and, if needed, a truck roll. Timestamps are what make the count count.

MAC Address

The modem the port currently sees.

Is it the modem you expect? A familiar MAC proves the right premises is on the right pair; a strange one after works in the area means a jumpering mix-up at the node.

DSL Mode

The modulation the port and modem negotiated.

VDSL2, always, on FTTN/B. Anything else (an ADSL fallback) means the CPE is misconfigured or negotiating badly, and every other number will be wrong until that's fixed.

Estimated Distance to Node

nbn's estimate of copper length, derived from attenuation. Indicative, not a measured run.

As the line's ceiling. Under ~200 m: 100 Mbps tier territory. 200 to 400 m: 60 to 100 Mbps. 400 to 700 m: 25 to 60 Mbps. Past 700 m: often can't hold 25 Mbps. Everything else in the result should make sense in light of this number.

why

Attenuation grows with distance and frequency, and VDSL2 leans on high frequencies, so distance dominates what the line can carry. The bands are community calibration, and co-existence drags each one down. If the estimate wildly disagrees with where the node visibly is, suspect the copper path (joints, bridged taps), not the map.

Physical Profile e.g. 100/40 Standard 6dB (Co-Existence)

Three facts packed into one string: the speed tier, the DLM state, the spectrum state.

Left to right: the tier you're checking against; then Standard 6dB means a clean history, while Stability, 9dB or 12dB means the line has been dropping and DLM or a tech intervened; then (Co-Existence) describes the area, not a fault.

why

DLM watches errors and resyncs, and moves misbehaving lines toward higher target margins, interleaving and retransmission, each step trading speed for staying up. So the profile string is the line's disciplinary record. Co-existence means legacy ADSL/special services still share the copper, so the node runs restricted spectrum; it mostly costs long lines, barely matters on short ones, and some areas never exit it.

Attainable Rate

What the line could sync at right now, given its length, noise and profile. The line's potential.

Against the tier. Comfortably above it: the copper supports the plan with room to spare. Just above: thin, watch it. Below the tier: the line physically cannot deliver the plan, and that's a tier/refund conversation with the RSP, not an nbn fault.

why

Under ACCC speed-claims guidance, RSPs must check attainable after activation and offer remedies (lower tier with refund, or exit) when a line can't reach the plan. nbn itself only commits to floors far below the tiers: 12/1 Mbps on FTTN during co-existence, 25/5 Mbps after. That's why "it's within spec" and "your 100/40 does 40" can both be true. The exception that IS a fault: attainable degrading against the line's own history, so screenshot baselines while things are healthy.

Attenuation Average often NaN or N/A

An aggregate counter the DSLAM frequently doesn't report.

Skip it. NaN, N/A, and unit glitches like "dBdB" or attenuation in "Mbps" are portal quirks, not line problems. Loop Attenuation and the distance estimate carry the same information reliably.

Current Sync Rate

The rate the port and modem actually negotiated. What the line is doing, as opposed to what it could do.

Against the cap, not the tier: nbn caps sync roughly 10% above the tier (100/40 syncs near 110/44 Mbps, 50/20 near 55/22 Mbps) to cover overheads. At the cap: the full plan is available on the copper, and slowness lives in the premises. Well below both the cap and the attainable: the port likely synced during a noise event; a port reset re-rolls the dice.

why

DSL framing and Ethernet overheads eat a slice of the raw sync, so nbn provisions the extra ~10% to make the tier deliverable at layer 3. The gap between a big attainable and a capped sync isn't lost speed; it's the DSLAM deliberately holding the line at the provisioned rate, and the spare capacity shows up in the noise margin instead.

Loop Attenuation

Signal loss across the copper loop, in dB. Distance in disguise.

Lower is better, and short loops read in single-digit dB. Under 20 dB is excellent territory; in the 40 dB range you're in low-tier country; past 60 dB the line is barely viable. If attenuation looks high for the known distance, the copper path itself (corroded joints, bridged taps) is suspect.

Noise Margin also SNR margin

The safety buffer between the received signal and the noise floor, in dB.

Against the profile's target, never in isolation. Near target: the line is working at its limit. Far above: headroom, usually because sync is capped. Under 6 dB and sinking: the line is fighting; expect drops, and compare a business-hours read to an evening one, because a margin that sags at night is noise ingress, not distance.

why

The DSLAM tunes sync to hold the target margin, so margin and sync are two ends of one trade. A capped line banks its surplus as margin (17+ dB on a short loop is normal and excellent). REIN from pumps, lights and dodgy power supplies runs on a schedule, which is why the day/night comparison is diagnostic.

Dropout history 24 h and 10 day graphs

Stability graphs that accompany the snapshot, showing resyncs over time.

Read the shape, not the count. Evening clusters: noise on a schedule (REIN from pumps, lights, dodgy power supplies). Random scatter: an intermittent physical fault, often a joint. A step change after rain: water in the pit or the pair. Weeks of quiet with one bad day reads completely differently from daily drops, even at the same total.

Read the result as one story: distance sets the ceiling (attainable), the profile caps the ask (sync), the spare becomes the buffer (margin), and the history shows in the profile string. When one field breaks that story, that field is your lead.

Stability Profile

FTTN / FTTBservice impacting

Not a measurement: an intervention. Applies a more conservative profile (higher target margin, interleaving, retransmission) that trades speed for holding sync.

Applying it

The line resyncs onto the new profile; sync rates usually come down.

Expect the speed drop and say so before applying, or the stability fix comes back as a speed complaint. Then leave it alone: the result is read over 48 hours, not on the next Line State.

After 48 hours

Rerun Line State and read Service Stability with the profile in place.

STABLE now: the line needed the margin; decide whether the speed trade is worth keeping or ask whether it can step back. Still not STABLE: you've done the step nbn expects first, so escalate to an instability fault with the before/after in hand.

Loopback

ALL TECHservice impacting

Sends a small burst of data from the network side to the premises equipment and back.

Result Passed / Failed

Whether data made the round trip at test time.

Passed proves exactly one thing: the path worked at that moment. It says nothing about speed, noise, or the drops that happen every evening, so it never closes a dropout investigation. Failed while the line shows in sync is the interesting contradiction: power cycle both ends, rerun, then escalate.

Created / Completed

Timestamps for the run.

A minute or two apart is normal, and the service can blip while it runs. A test stuck incomplete usually means the far end never answered; treat like a fail.

Connection tests

ALL TECH

The blunt instruments. Each one answers a smaller question than techs usually credit it with.

Check Connection

Whether the service looks online from the provider's side. No impact to the service.

Connected answers only "is the session up right now". Not Connected: power cycle the nbn equipment (30 seconds off), retest, and escalate if it still shows Not Connected, especially if a loopback fails too. Run this before anything heavier; it's the zeroth question.

Kick Connection service impacting

Not a test: clears the current session so the CPE can reconnect fresh. Disconnects immediately; reconnection can take up to 10 minutes.

Use it around modem swaps and stuck sessions. Read the aftermath, not the kick: a new modem that hasn't come up 10 minutes after a kick is an escalation (auth or port), not a retry.

Port Reset FTTP · FTTC · FTTN/B service impacting

Locks and unlocks the UNI-D or node port to force a clean reconnection.

The move for "port up but no data" and for a sync sitting oddly below attainable. Read two things: a status of Rejected after the test is itself escalation-worthy, and a problem that clears then returns points at the premises side, because the port keeps getting re-broken from your end of the wire.

NTD Status

HFC

Operational check on the coax connection box. On HFC you can't see RF levels or segment noise; the flap counter is your window.

Status

Whether the NTD is online and registered.

Fail with power present, after a reset and ten minutes, is nbn's problem. While you wait, check the premises coax: fly lead, wall plate, and especially old pay TV splitters, the classic silent killer of HFC signal.

Flap Count

How often the NTD's connection has interrupted and re-registered.

Never as one number; always as a delta. Take a reading, wait 24 hours, read again. Five or more new flaps a day apart is instability evidence worth a fault, and it lines up with the 4-in-24h Performance Incident threshold.

NTD Reset service impacting

Forces the box to reboot and re-register on the network.

The first move for errors from the status test. Read it after ten minutes, not immediately: still reporting problems after a reset plus the wait is nbn's fault to chase, not another reset.

DPU / NCD Status

FTTC

One fact explains most FTTC results: the pit unit (DPU) is reverse powered by the NCD inside the premises, over the copper pair.

DPU Status

Whether the kerb equipment is online.

Offline means power path first: the NCD is off, faulty, or the pair between them is broken. It does not mean "the internet is down"; it means the pit has no electricity.

DPU Port Status shows data rate

Power delivery from the NCD, plus the sync rate on the short lead-in.

The copper run is tens of metres, so the rate should sit near the tier's maximum. A low rate on a lead-in this short is genuinely abnormal: escalation-worthy after a port reset, because it points at the pair or a joint.

NCD Status + flaps

The same status-and-flap-counter reading as HFC's NTD, on the FTTC connection box.

Read flaps as a delta, a day apart, same as HFC: five or more new ones is instability evidence. On FTTC also cross-check the DPU: flapping here with a healthy DPU points inside the premises; flapping with DPU power complaints points at the pair.

NCD UNI-D Status

The Ethernet port your router plugs into.

Up but no data flowing: port reset, then retest. Down: the cable and the router end before anything nbn-side.

DPU Port Reset service impacting

Resets the pit-side port feeding the premises.

Use after an offline status or a low data rate, then reread DPU Port Status. Rate restored to near max: transient, note and move on. Still low on copper this short: escalate; that's a pair or joint problem nbn owns.

UNI-D Status

FTTP

Full fibre has no line quality to argue about from the portal; optical levels are nbn-side. What you can read is the data port.

MAC Address

The router the provisioned port can see.

A MAC showing means the NTD sees your router; from there, slowness is almost never the access line, so look at CPE throughput, Wi-Fi and config. No MAC across two different routers means the port or the provisioning, and remember only one of the four UNI-D ports is live.

Fixed Wireless

FW

No line state equivalent exists; you read patterns instead of ports.

Connection box lights

The indoor unit's power and status LEDs.

Green is normal. Amber or red power, or red status, is a fault state for the provider. No lights is the power path.

The speed pattern

Speeds sampled morning, afternoon and evening across a few days. The pattern is the diagnostic.

Fine all day, poor in the evening: cell congestion, a capacity complaint, not an equipment fault. Poor at all hours: signal path, so think grown trees, new buildings, a knocked antenna, damaged cable. Tracks the weather: radio path evidence worth handing to nbn.

After the read

Once you've done the read yourself, these are the sentences that make it count.

What to say to the RSP, by finding

Read: unstable.

"Line State shows the service as UNSTABLE with [N] resyncs. Please apply a stability profile; I will retest in 48 hours and want an nbn instability fault raised if it has not returned to STABLE."

Read: dropouts past the PI threshold.

"I have logged [N] dropouts in 24 hours with timestamps, which exceeds nbn's Performance Incident threshold of 4 unexpected dropouts per day. Please raise it with nbn as a Performance Incident."

Read: attainable below the tier.

"Attainable is [X/Y] on a [tier] service. The line cannot support the tier, so please run the speed review process: I want the ACCC remedy options (tier move with refund), and an nbn investigation only if this is a degradation from previous attainable figures."

Read: degraded against its own baseline.

"Attainable was [A/B] on [date] (screenshot attached) and is now [C/D] on the same profile. That is a physical degradation of the line, please lodge it with nbn."

Read: healthy line, slow experience. Say nothing to nbn. The access line is exonerated; work the premises: wired test at the modem, CPE throughput, Wi-Fi, cabling, the measuring device.

The habit that wins arguments: baselines

Snapshot results while services are healthy: at activation, after any nbn work, and at every suspected fault. A line measurably worse than its own baseline is the single most persuasive artifact you can hand an RSP; a single bad snapshot is dismissible. For suspected noise, capture one business-hours read and one evening read and compare margins.
